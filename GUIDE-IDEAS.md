### Writing rules
- 5-minute read at most: about 1,000 words in total. Intro under 80 words, each habit under 120, close under 60.
- Same shape for every habit: name, the idea in one sentence, one picture from everyday life, one "try this week". Nothing else.
- At most one number per habit.
- Short sentences and plain words, explained like to a 5-year-old (but not literally). Define any jargon the first time it appears.

## Source notes: showing up (front door + goals vs systems)

Two of the three original ideas merged, because they are the same argument in two steps: getting started is the hard part, and a routine is what gets you started every time.

Core lines
- "The heaviest weight in the gym is the front door." Attributed to Ed Latimore (former boxer, author); also shared by James Clear.
- "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." James Clear, Atomic Habits.
- When goals and daily habits disagree, the habits win.

Points to cover
- The mental barrier (self-doubt, fear of being judged, putting it off, low motivation) is heavier than the physical work. Once you are through the door, the hardest part is behind you.
- Discipline over motivation: waiting to "feel like it" rarely works. Build a routine whose only job is to get you there.
- Goal = destination, system = vehicle. A goal says where; the system is what you do each day.
- Goals win once, systems win repeatedly. Motivation often drops after the target is hit; the routine keeps going.
- Motivation fades, habits run on autopilot.
- Small daily gains compound; forcing one big leap usually ends in burnout.

How to close the gap (the practical part)
1. Audit the real daily loop: what you actually do from morning to night, not what you hope to do.
2. Trigger routines: tie a tiny new habit to something you already do ("After I pour my morning coffee, I will write down my top priority for the day").
3. Lower friction for good habits (shoes by the door, book on the pillow).
4. Raise friction for bad habits (make them annoying to reach).

Padel angle to explore
- The heaviest thing in the padel bag is the car door: booking the court and turning up.
- Goal "reach level X" versus system "two drilling sessions and one match every week".
- Possible numbers tie-in with Guide 03/04: sessions attended versus rating over time.

Section idea: athlete types (added 16 Nov 2026, rough)

Premise: "Berlatih seperti bertanding, bertanding seperti berlatih." English options for the page:
- "Train like it's the final. Play the final like it's training."
- "Sweat in practice as if it counts. Play when it counts as if it's practice."
- "Make practice a battle, so the battle feels like practice."

Vitto's two types
- A. The Spartan (volume). Trains relentlessly, their whole life, so that when the battle comes it feels like a "vacation". Example: Michael Phelps, who by his own account went years without a rest day.
- B. The Engineer (precision). Cuts the hours and makes each one count. Example: Cam McEvoy, who redesigned his sprint training around strength and short, race-speed efforts, cut his pool hours sharply (Vitto's memory: about 20 hours down to about 6) and won the 50 m freestyle at the top level.

More types to consider (Claude's suggestions, pick or drop)
- C. The Metronome (polarised). Most sessions deliberately easy, a few very hard, intensity measured rather than felt. Example: Norwegian endurance athletes such as Jakob Ingebrigtsen and Kristian Blummenfelt.
- D. The Sampler (late specialiser). Plays many sports young, picks one late, brings the borrowed skills along. Example: Roger Federer, as told in David Epstein's "Range", against Tiger Woods as the early specialiser.
- E. The Player (learns by playing). Little formal drilling, huge amounts of small-sided games. Example: Brazilian footballers raised on futsal and street football.
- F. The Long-Gamer (recovery first). Treats sleep, food and rest as part of training to stay at the top for decades. Example: LeBron James, Novak Djokovic.

Padel angle
- Which type are you now, and which does your week actually allow? A club player with a job and four court hours a week cannot be a Spartan, so those hours have to be Engineer hours.
- Possible numbers tie-in: Vitto's own load data (Vitto Padel Hours, hard-after-hard days).

Caveats to state in the guide
- The event picks the method. A 50 m swim is about 21 seconds of pure power; Phelps raced 200 m and 400 m events that need endurance. McEvoy's plan would not have worked for Phelps's races, and the reverse.
- Padel is a skill sport, so cutting hours only works if the remaining hours hold more good repetitions, not just fewer.
- We only hear about the athletes each method worked for (survivorship bias: the ones it broke do not get documentaries).

To verify before publishing
- McEvoy's exact before and after training hours, and whether his 50 m result was a world record or a world/Olympic title. Claude recalls a 2023 world title, 2024 Olympic gold and a world record in 2026, but has not checked.
- "Matthew Smith": Claude could not place this name in the McEvoy story (his coach is recalled as Tim Lane). Confirm who was meant.
- Phelps's "no days off" stretch: length and years.

Caveat to state in the guide
- "1% better every day" (about 37x in a year) is arithmetic used as a picture, not a measured result. Skill does not compound that cleanly.

## Source notes: comparison (thief of joy vs teacher of skill)

Core line
- Comparison is the thief of joy when applied broadly, and the teacher of skill when applied narrowly. (James Clear: "to improve, compare little things".)

Broad comparison: thief of joy
- Comparing your whole life, worth or path to someone else's brings insecurity and anxiety.
- Examples to avoid: career and job titles, relationship status, net worth and lifestyle.
- Why it hurts: you compare your behind-the-scenes (doubts, day one) with their highlight reel (day 90, curated). It measures your worth, not your path.

Narrow comparison: teacher of skill
- Look at how someone handles one small, specific task and you get a blueprint.
- Examples: exercise form, writing tactics, a marketing move, a coding habit.
- Swap "Why are they better than me?" for "What exact thing are they doing that I can test?" Envy becomes data.

Summary table

| Dimension | Broad (thief of joy) | Narrow (teacher of skill) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Whole life, status, identity | One habit, tool or technique |
| Question | "Why is their life better than mine?" | "How do they do this one step?" |
| Result | Envy, paralysis, dissatisfaction | Feedback, iteration, growth |

- Narrow: how one player prepares the bandeja, where their feet are on the lob return, their grip on the volley.

Guide 03 · The numbers

Nobody wins padel. People lose it.

I charted 23 sets, point by point, to see what separates the pair that wins from the pair that loses. It was not who hit the best shots. It was who gave away the fewest.

00 · The count

Every point goes in one of two boxes.

When a point ends, I ask one question: did somebody win it, or did somebody give it away?

A winner is a shot the other pair could not get back. That includes a shot so good it forced the miss.

An unforced error is a miss nobody forced. The ball was playable and it went into the net, the glass or out.

There is no third box. Every point is one or the other, so the two always add up to the whole set.

More than half the points in these matches were not won. They were handed over.

01 · The hard line

Fewer errors won every set I charted.

In 20 of the 23 sets, one pair made fewer unforced errors than the other. I checked who won those sets.

20 of 20
sets won by the pair with fewer unforced errors

Not most of them. All of them, in this sample. No pair won a set after making more errors than the other side.

Here is why. Winners were almost equal: pairs that won a set hit 14.2 winners on average, and pairs that lost hit 13.1. Errors were not equal at all: 11.6 against 17.9. At this level pairs differ in what they give away, not in what they create.

Sets inside one match are not independent, because it is the same four people on the same day. So I also checked whole matches: the pair with fewer errors won 10 of 10.

In the other 3 sets the errors were level. Only then did winners decide it: the pair with more winners took all 3.

Think of a golf scorecard.
The club medal is rarely won by the player with the most birdies. It is won by the player with the fewest double bogeys.

02 · A bird in the hand

Winners cannot buy back errors.

A winner and an error are each worth one point on the scoreboard. They are not worth the same over a set. One error cost more than one winner earned.

1.4
winners needed to cancel one unforced error

Put simply: miss three balls and you need about four winners just to get back to where you started.

So can you make more mistakes if you also hit more winners? Eight times in this sample a pair tried exactly that: more winners than the other side, and more errors too. None of the eight won the set.

The clearest case is on the scale. One pair hit 13 more winners than their opponents in a single set. They also made 12 more errors. All that attacking bought them one extra point, and they lost the set 6–7.

A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush.
The ball you keep in play is the bird in your hand. The winner you go for is still in the bush.

03 · The most expensive miss

The volley you miss costs more than the one you make.

A volley is a ball you hit before it bounces, usually close to the net. Up there you are closest to winning the point. You are also closest to giving it away.

In 19 sets, one pair missed more volleys than the other.

1 of 19
sets won by the pair with more volley errors

The one exception still made 7 fewer errors overall than their opponents, so they stayed on the right side of the hard line.

Now the surprise. Hitting more volley winners did not help. The pair with more volley winners won only 6 of 18 sets. Pairs that went for more at the net usually missed more there too.

Think of the short putt.
Nobody claps for a tap-in, but a three-putt wrecks the card. The volley is your short putt: the hard work was getting there.

1 / 19sets won with more volley errors than the other pair
6 / 18sets won with more volley winners than the other pair

A missed volley is also an unforced error, so this overlaps with chapter 01. Read it as the place where the error gap most often shows up, not as a separate law. Sets where the two pairs were level are left out of each count.

04 · Where attacking does pay

Overheads win sets, but only behind the line.

An overhead is any ball you hit above your head: the bandeja, the víbora, the smash, and the bajada off the back glass.

In 22 sets, one pair hit more overhead winners than the other.

17 of 22
sets won by the pair with more overhead winners

So this is where attacking pays. Now look at the 5 pairs who hit more overhead winners and still lost. Every one of them had made at least 7 more errors than their opponents.

Overheads never rescued a pair that was on the wrong side of the error line.

Sambal and rice.
Good sambal makes a plate of rice great. It does not save burnt rice. Keeping the ball in is the rice. The overhead is the sambal.

One caution on cause and effect. A pair that is losing tends to send up weaker lobs, and weak lobs become easy overheads. So some of these overhead winners may be a result of being ahead, not the reason for it.

05 · The reverse stress test

Start from the loss and work backwards.

A reverse stress test is a tool from finance. A normal plan asks what could go wrong. This one starts from the thing that already went wrong and asks: what would it have taken to avoid it?

Late for a 7 pm match.
You do not ask "was there traffic?" There is always traffic. You ask "what time should I have left?"

For a lost set the question becomes: how many fewer errors would have put us on the winning side of the line?

I ran it on my own four sets in this sample: a Padel Pro Gold final and a Gold round of 16. I lost all four.

The last one stings most. Errors were level at 20 each and winners were 12 against 13. One fewer miss and we are on the other side of the line.

In all four sets, at least half of my pair's errors were mine. That is my homework, not my partner's.

Taking away an error does not hand you the point. It keeps the rally alive, and you still have to play it. Read the flip number as the distance to the line, not as a promise.

06 · Your turn

Chart one set of your own.

A pen, a grid and one set to watch is enough. Video helps, because you can rewind. My template is below if you want a head start.

  1. Fix everyone's place.Left-side player on the left, right-side player on the right. The pair you are following on top, the opponents below. Keep it the same all match.
  2. Note who is serving.Each game, mark which pair serves and which pair returns.
  3. Watch the point to its end.Then ask the one question: was it won, or was it given away?
  4. Put one mark in one box.Whose racket, and winner or error. Add the shot type if you want more detail.
  5. Add it up, then run the stress test.Count the errors for each pair. The gap plus one is your distance to the line.

In short

The whole guide in six lines.

  1. 52%Half of all points are given away. More points end in a miss nobody forced than in a winner.
  2. 20 of 20Fewer errors won every set in this sample. 20 of 20 sets, and 10 of 10 matches.
  3. 1.4 to 1Winners cannot buy back errors. One error took about 1.4 winners to cancel, and more winners plus more errors lost 8 times out of 8.
  4. 1 of 19The missed volley costs the most. Hitting more volley winners did not make up for it.
  5. 17 of 22Overheads pay, but only behind the line. They never rescued a pair that was losing on errors.
  6. Gap + 1Work backwards from a loss. Your error gap plus one is how far you were from flipping the set.

Your next match

Three things to take on court.

1

One more ball.

When you are not sure the winner is there, put the ball back in play. Make them hit one more.

2

Safe volley, earned smash.

At the net, play the volley you know you will make. Keep the big swing for the high ball that is clearly yours.

3

Count after the set.

Ask one question: who gave away more points? If it was you, that number is your next training session.

Devil's advocate

The case against this guide.

A finding you cannot argue with is usually a finding nobody has tested. Here are the three strongest objections I know of, and how much of each I accept.

Objection 1

"You restated the scoreboard."

Every point is a winner or an error, so the points you win are your winners plus their errors. Saying the pair with fewer errors won is close to saying the team that let in fewer goals won.

Partly true. The error gap is built into the score. What the count adds is which half mattered: winners were almost equal between pairs, and errors were not.

Objection 2

"Unforced is your opinion."

One person decided which misses were unforced, already knowing who won. If the better pair's depth and speed caused those misses, then somebody did win the point. A stronger pair may simply do both: miss less and win.

True, and I cannot rule it out. This data shows that fewer errors and winning go together. It does not prove that one causes the other. Treat "make fewer errors" as something to test in your own game.

Objection 3

"It is 10 matches, not 23 sets."

The 23 sets come from 10 matches in one league over nine days. I also looked at 13 different stats, and chapters 03 and 04 show the most striking ones. Look at enough numbers and some will stand out by luck.

True. At match level the pattern holds 10 of 10, but with only 10 matches the data cannot rule out a real rate as low as about 7 in 10. The volley and overhead findings need a second sample before I trust them.

Disclosure

About the data.

If I show you numbers, you should be able to see exactly what is in them and what is not. Here is everything.

What was charted
24 sets from Indonesian Padel League matches. 23 have a final score and are used for every win or lose number in this guide. Those 23 sets come from 10 matches. One set was charted for scouting without a score and is left out of those numbers.
When
The 23 scored sets were played between 16 and 24 October 2026.
Who is in it
Players based in Indonesia and foreign players in the league, from high Silver level upward. That is roughly 4.0 to 6.5 on the international rating scale. All other players are anonymised.
Nothing left out
Every set I charted is included. I did not pick the ones that fit. Four of the sets are my own, and I lost all four. The main result barely moves if you remove them.
How points are counted
Each point is logged as a winner or an unforced error, by shot type and by whether the pair was serving or returning. There is no separate "forced error" box: a shot that forces a miss counts as a winner for the pair that hit it.
Set by set
I count each set on its own and combine sets into a match afterwards. That lets me see fatigue and changes of tactics between sets. Some coaches count a whole match as one, which is also valid and may lead to different conclusions.
What was tested
I compared 13 different stats against who won. This guide shows the ones that stood out, which is a reason to be careful with them. The full list is in the case against this guide, above.
Known limits
One person did all the charting: me. Whether a miss was forced is a judgement call, and some of my calls are wrong. The shot list is not complete either. A wall exit or a counter-smash, for example, gets filed under forehand, backhand, volley or smash.
Dates and source
Sets played 16 to 24 October 2026. Analysis built 7 November 2026. Page last updated 16 Nov 2026. Source: my own point-by-point charting; nothing here comes from a third party.
How to read it
23 sets is a small sample at one level of play. Read every number as a pattern worth testing, not a law. One set won by a pair that made 2 or more extra errors would break the main finding, and I check for that every time I chart a new batch.
